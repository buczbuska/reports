The core question
When the text of a rule and its purpose diverge — which do people follow? And which do observers expect, judge, and enforce?
Textualism: follow E, the explicitly prescribed option.
Purposivism: follow the outcome the rule aims to achieve (equal split).
We ask this for rule-followers (Exp 1 & 2) and for third-party enforcers (Exp 3) — and we dissociate two DVs: compliance concept vs. punitive enforcement.
Why it matters
Legal interpretation
Textualism vs. purposivism — a centuries-old jurisprudential debate (Scalia vs. Hart & Sacks). Recent empirical work shows people lean textualist by default (Almeida et al., 2023), but the conditions under which purposivism emerges remain poorly mapped.
Judgment vs. decision making
Does the same person apply the same interpretive logic when acting under a rule as when judging another's action? Prior work studies the two roles separately — we dissociate them in the same design.
Empirical gap
No study has orthogonally manipulated text vs. purpose while measuring both a compliance judgment DV and a punishment DV across both actor roles. We do all four simultaneously.
Theoretical background
Rule interpretation: a behavioural black box
Legal interpretation — and rule interpretation in general — has long been debated in normative terms (textualism vs. purposivism), but the psychological question of what people actually do has remained surprisingly understudied.
Almeida et al. (2023)
Participants generally rely more on the textual meaning of a rule than on its purpose when both are available. The prescription label is the default anchor.
Hannikainen et al. (2022)
The textual bias is amplified in coordination contexts — when participants must align with others, the public salience of the rule's letter makes it the natural focal point.
Bystranowski et al. (2024)
Researchers link the textual dominance to public salience: the rule's written text is cognitively prominent and provides a coordination device that overrides purpose-reasoning.
The judgment–decision gap & our contribution
Judgment vs. decision making: Interpreting a rule to evaluate someone else's behaviour (judgment) may recruit different processes than deciding how to act under the same rule. The compliance concept and punitive enforcement may follow different logics.
Perspective matters: Which interpretive method feels natural may depend on whether you are the rule-follower (internalising purpose) or an outside evaluator (relying on the visible text).
Consequences sharpen purposivism: When a decision is pointless or unjust, relying on textual meaning becomes harder to maintain — moral cost activates purpose-reasoning even for textualists.
Our contribution: Orthogonal text/purpose manipulation · dual DVs (judgment + punishment) · three actor roles across three experiments. First design to dissociate text from purpose empirically at both levels simultaneously.
The fundamental tension
A rule says: "Choose option E."   The stated purpose: "Achieve an equal split."
Text = Purpose
E = 50–50 · Normal
No tension. Follow the rule, achieve the purpose. Unambiguous baseline.
Text ≠ Purpose
E = 30–70 · Underinclusion
Prescription fails the goal. Text says E; purpose wants 50–50. They cannot both be satisfied.
Text ⊕ Purpose
E = 100–0 · Selfish
Prescription is morally objectionable. Text says E; fairness norm demands otherwise.
Key design move: We need conditions where the textually-compliant choice and the purpose-compliant choice are different options with different payoffs — so any C ≈ E result cannot be dismissed as outcome identity.

Why IdenticalToE?
In Over, C and E both pay 50–50. Any C = E result in deduction might reflect evaluators who cannot justify different punishment for identical outcomes — not genuine purpose-compliance leniency.
IdenticalToE isolates this payoff-identity confound.
The OverUnder condition resolves it entirely: C = 50–50 ≠ E = 30–70 (different payoffs), yet C ≈ E in evaluation. If C ≈ E in OverUnder, the purpose-compliance effect is real.

Interpreting the results
OR = 0.020–0.027 (Under, OverUnder): Over 97% reduction in odds of choosing E. These are among the largest effect sizes achievable in a binary-choice paradigm — underinclusion devastates adherence.
Over, UnderSelfish, and OverUnderSelfish all non-significant. The critical driver of deviation is specifically purpose-failure in underinclusion — not the mere presence of an alternative, not self-interest alone. Participants follow the letter even when selfishness would also justify E.
Why blme? Adherence rates above 85% in Normal/Over create quasi-complete separation — standard lme4 produces infinite or unstable log-ORs. blme::bglmer() with a weakly informative normal prior (SD = 3 on the log-OR scale) regularises estimates without changing the substantive conclusions.

Experiment 3 — third-party judgment design
Task
Independent evaluators (not involved in allocation) were shown all nine options A–I in their assigned condition and asked for each:
1
Likert: "How much does this choice comply with the rule?" (6-point: Definitely violates → Definitely complies)
2
Deduction: How many of 50 points would you deduct from this person?
Between-subjects for condition (N = 290 total; ~48/condition); within-subjects for all nine options A–I. Each evaluator provides 9 Likert ratings and 9 deduction amounts within their assigned condition.
Both DVs for every option — so each evaluator gives 9 Likert ratings and 9 deduction amounts, enabling within-person correlation.
Why two DVs?
Likert = compliance concept. How does the evaluator interpret what the rule requires? This is a labelling judgment.
Deduction = punitive enforcement. Is the evaluator willing to use their punishment power? This is a behavioural commitment.
These two DVs can — and do — dissociate. A person can simultaneously say "they followed the rule" (high Likert) and "I will punish them" (high deduction). This occurs in 44% of evaluators in the most extreme condition (OverUnderSelfish) — a genuine within-person logical inversion that is invisible if you measure only one DV. Mean within-person r = 0.26 across the whole sample.

The E_Role framework — a cleaner decomposition
Why Role coding?
The standard LMM assigns β_Text partly from Textual_Equal conditions (E in Normal/Over), where E is both TextCompliant and PurposeCompliant. This inflates β_Text by absorbing the combined bonus.
Role coding assigns each option one mutually exclusive credential — so we can ask directly: pure-text vs. pure-purpose.
If Textual_Pure = Equal_Only on both DVs, there is no text–purpose asymmetry and the apparent gap in the standard LMM is an artefact.

Synthesis — five central findings
Finding 1 — Textualism dominates the compliance concept. Likert tracks the prescribed letter E across all six conditions — even when E prescribes a morally objectionable outcome. E rated 1.7 pts above C in Over despite identical payoffs (d = 1.02). β_Text = +1.99*** vs. β_Purpose = +1.68*** in non-selfish LMM. Role analysis shows Textual_Pure ≈ Equal_Only (Δ = 0.14, p = .97) — the apparent gap is a Textual_Equal artefact, not a universal text premium.
Finding 2 — Consequentialism governs punishment. Deduction tracks outcomes, not the rule label. β_Text (−5.20) ≈ β_Purpose (−5.06): text and purpose protect equally from punishment. Even non-compliant generous options (G/H/I) escape punishment in non-selfish conditions — an altruism heuristic not predicted by any prior model. No text > purpose asymmetry in enforcement.
Finding 3 — Selfish reversal: enforcement inverts, concept does not. In selfish conditions (E = 100–0), β_Text flips to +6.95*** (prescribed selfish E now punished more); β_Purpose = −6.01*** (equal-split still protected). Simultaneously, Likert shows no reversal: evaluators still rate E as most compliant. Two opposite logics operate within the same evaluator in the same task.
Finding 4 — OverUnder equivalence: purpose fully substitutes for text. C (purpose-only, 50–50) = E (text-only, 30–70) on both DVs (both p = 1.000), despite different payoffs and different compliance credentials. Under combined rule ambiguity, the letter and the spirit are evaluatively interchangeable.
Finding 5 — Within-person dissociation is pervasive and individual. Pooled mean within-person r = 0.26 (SD = 0.47) between Likert and deduction across 9 options. In OverUnderSelfish, 44% of evaluators show a negative r — they personally rate E more compliant while giving E more deduction points. This is not an aggregate artefact; it is a genuine individual-level logical inversion.

The dual-process account
Process 1 — Prescriptive Identification
Governs the compliance concept (Likert).
Identifies the prescribed option E as the compliance anchor
Robust across all conditions — operates even when E is morally objectionable
Does not reverse under selfish prescriptions
Default: "What does the rule say? It says E."
Process 2 — Outcome / Moral Evaluation
Governs punitive enforcement (deduction).
Asks: what was the actual outcome? Was it fair?
Equal-split options consistently escape punishment
Reverses under selfish prescriptions: punishes E, protects C
Active: "What did they actually do to the other person?"
These processes coexist within individuals. Mean within-person r = 0.26 — related but separable. Under competing normative pressures, they point in opposite directions and individuals apply both simultaneously.

Legal interpretation literature
Almeida et al. (2023)
People default to textual interpretation when rule text and purpose are available. We confirm this in behaviour (Exp 1 & 2) and compliance judgment (Exp 3 Likert) — and extend it by showing where and why purposivism emerges.
Hannikainen et al. (2022) · Bystranowski et al. (2024)
Coordination and public salience amplify textual bias. Our Exp 2 (anticipated punishment = public scrutiny) raises adherence, consistent with text salience under observation.
Judgment vs. decision making distinction
The dual-DV design confirms the judgment/decision split: compliance concept (Likert) is textualist; punishment (deduction) is consequentialist. The same person applies different logics to different domains of evaluation.
Both interpretive orientations are psychologically real — not competing theories about what people should do, but descriptions of two separable cognitive processes operating simultaneously.
Limitations
1. Payoff-identity confound (Over): C and E both = 50–50; deduction null may partly reflect outcome-identity leniency rather than pure purpose-compliance. OverUnder addresses this — same null with different payoffs.
2. Altruism heuristic not pre-specified: G/H/I leniency in non-selfish conditions was not a primary hypothesis. Exploratory — future work should orthogonally manipulate generosity and compliance status.
3. Self-interest confound (selfish conditions): E = 100–0 maximises personal gain. Elevated adherence may reflect self-interest, not rule-following per se. Cannot be cleanly separated in this design.
4. Order effects (Exp 3): Likert and deduction collected in the same session. Within-person item order not counterbalanced. Contrast or anchoring effects cannot be ruled out.
5. Between-subjects design (Exp 1 & 2): Each participant saw two conditions only. Lower power for interaction effects.
6. IA subsample: Inequality Aversion index valid only for Prosocials (~55–70% of samples). IA covariate analyses based on self-selected subset; reduced external validity for that specific finding.
Implications & future directions
Theoretical implications
Dissociable processes
Rule interpretation and rule enforcement are separable cognitive processes — not a single "compliance" construct. Designing studies with only one DV will miss this split.
Consequentialist enforcement
Punishment is morally consequentialist: people protect equal-split outcomes even when the rule prescribes selfishness. Enforcement is not mechanically rule-driven.
Prescription as independent cue
The rule has evaluative power that cannot be reduced to outcomes or purposes (it's complicated).
Future directions
Within-subjects design: Can individuals consciously recognise that their compliance judgments and punishment decisions contradict each other? Confronting evaluators with their own inconsistency could test whether the dissociation is reflectively endorsed or driven by automatic processes.
Institutional contexts: Do the same dual-process dynamics replicate with employment contracts, tax regulations, or real legal judgment tasks where participants have domain expertise? Expertise may amplify textualism (legal training) or purposivism (professional ethics).
Motivated purposivism: Is purposivist deviation strategic (self-serving when purpose and self-interest happen to align) or genuinely normative (people care about the rule's goal)? Orthogonally varying whether the purpose-achieving option benefits oneself or a third party would cleanly disentangle these motives.
Summary
Behaviour (Exp 1 & 2)
Rule-subjects are predominantly textualist — they follow E. Underinclusion erodes this; anticipated punishment restores it selectively in conditions where purposivist deviation was greatest. SVO & IA: largely non-predictive. Ball task (general rule-following tendency): significantly predicts behavioral adherence in Exp 1 & 2 (OR = 1.58 per SD, p < .001) but not third-party judgments in Exp 3 — dispositional rule compliance shapes behavior, not evaluation.
Compliance concept (Likert)
Third parties rate the prescribed E as most compliant across all conditions — even when E is morally objectionable. The prescription label governs the compliance concept.
Enforcement (Deduction)
Punishment tracks outcomes and fairness, not the prescription label. Equal-split options escape punishment equally to E. Under selfish prescriptions, enforcement inverts entirely.
Textualism for the compliance concept.   Purposivism for punishment.
